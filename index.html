⚙️ Keep the two-stroke, or repower?

Our Yamaha V6 200 is a 1990s carburetted two-stroke. Modern outboards are four-strokes (or direct-injection two-strokes). Why people call a four-stroke an "upgrade":

Our V6 200 2-strokeModern 150hp 4-stroke
Fuel at cruise~35–42 L/hr~18–22 L/hr
OilBurns 2-stroke oil as it goes (~$15–25 a trip)Sump oil, changed at service
Smoke, smell, noiseSmoky at idle, loudQuiet enough to talk over
Reliability30 years old; ignition parts getting scarceNew, warranty, fuel injected — starts every time
Weight~180 kg~215–220 kg (heavier!)
PunchLots — 2-strokes are strong for their weightSmooth; 150hp is plenty for this hull
CostAlready on the boat$26–30k fitted (F150) · $34–38k (F200)

The maths: saving ~18 L/hr × ~30 hours a year × ~$2.20/L ≈ $1,200 a year in fuel (plus oil). Against a ~$28k repower, that's a 20+ year payback — and a new motor adds far less to resale than it costs.

Our call: it's an option, not a plan. If the V6 sparks up with good compression, run it — budget fuel at ~$150–200 a day out. Only repower if the mechanic says it's cooked (or the ignition parts can't be found). Middle path: a good second-hand 115–150hp four-stroke ($8–15k) if it comes to that.

📜 The builder

International Marine of Scoresby is a genuine Victorian boatbuilding dynasty. Founded in 1958 by Arch Spooner, it built Bertrams under licence from 1963 and sold its own trailer boats as Caribbean — the badge on our hull. Barry Spooner ran it from 1971; grandson Richard runs it today, on the same Ferntree Gully Road site. Roughly 50,000–60,000 boats built. Caribbeans have a reputation as tough, conservative, sea-kindly boats that hold their value.

The factory is reportedly helpful with build details if you quote the serial: (03) 9763 7233, quote "Model P209, serial 2513". That's our best shot at the build year and an original brochure.

🔍 Before we hand over money — inspect

  1. Transom: motor trimmed down, push and pull the leg hard — any flex or cracking around the bolts? Tap inside with a small hammer: a dull thud means wet timber.
  2. Floor: walk the cockpit, especially near the tank hatch. Spongy = rot.
  3. Stringers/foam: heavier than expected on a weighbridge = waterlogged foam.
  4. Old sterndrive cut-out? Look for a patched hole in the transom.
  5. Hardtop & windows: hose test — leaks at the hardtop join and sliding windows; stains in the cabin.
  6. Fuel tank: old aluminium underfloor tanks corrode. Sender, lines, water separator, and the age of the fuel.
  7. Trailer: springs, brakes, bearings, rollers, rust at the drawbar, the ATM plate.
  8. Motor: once it sparks — compression test (all 6 within ~10%), oil injection pump & warning buzzer, tell-tale stream.

🎓 Marine licence
  • Who: the person in charge of a powered boat (over 5hp). Passengers don't need one — legally one licensed skipper is enough, but the boat sits idle if only one of us can drive it.
  • How: do an approved course (online is quickest — some are free, some ~$100–150), sit the knowledge test ($33.70), then take your certificate to VicRoads/Transport Victoria.
  • Fee: $42 for 1 year or $210 for 5 years. New licences come in 1 or 5 year terms.
  • Easiest path: all do the online course the same week, sit the test, one trip to a VicRoads centre.
  • VicRoads — marine licence
🎣 Recreational fishing licence
  • Everyone who fishes needs their own (it's per angler, not per boat). 2026–27 online prices: 3-day $10 · 28-day $24.70 · 1-year $40.80 · 3-year $111.20.
  • Buy instantly at service.vic.gov.au/fishing or the VicFishing app — it lives on your phone. Exemptions apply for some (age, concessions) verify.
  • Tip: the 3-year licence is cheapest per year — and part of the fee funds fish stocking and better ramps.
🛥️ Boat registration
  • Every powered boat on Victorian waters must be registered, renewed annually (~$104.50 for powered vessels over 4m verify).
  • Transfer: Uncle Uncle (seller) and our nominated owner both sign the Vessel Registration Transfer form; lodge with VicRoads within 14 days with the $21.50 fee.
  • Paperwork: a signed bill of sale (price + date — even if it's mates' rates), current rego number, HIN if available.
  • PPSR search on the HIN or motor serial at ppsr.gov.au — checks no finance is owing. Even with family, it's a few dollars well spent.
  • No stamp duty on boats found in Victoria verify.
🚚 Trailer registration
  • Light trailers (under 4.5t) transfer for $7.20 in a private sale; no roadworthy needed. Annual trailer rego is separate from the boat rego.
  • While you're there: check the trailer's compliance plate (ATM) — we need it for the towing maths.
🛡️ Insurance
  • Ballpark $500–1,200/yr for a $10–25k trailer cabin cruiser est. — get three quotes: Club Marine, BoatSafe, RACV Marine, NRMA Marine, Nautilus.
  • Ask for: agreed value, $5M+ on-water liability, cover while towing, salvage/towing, lay-up discount for winter.
  • Co-ownership: declare it. Name every owner (or list as "any licensed operator") so a claim can't be knocked back because Merv's son was driving.
  • Older boats may need a survey or a mechanic's report before cover — the recommission service doubles as that.

⚰️ The CoPCO Tontine (our co-op agreement)

The deal: last one standing owns the boat. No selling out, no buy-outs. When an owner dies, their share passes to the surviving owners. The final survivor gets the boat (and the fuel bill).

A tontine was originally a 17th-century investment scheme: everyone paid in, and the payouts were shared among the survivors until the last one took the lot. They're banned as financial products today, but the "last survivor takes all" idea is legal for jointly owned property. In Australia it's called joint tenancy with right of survivorship: when one joint owner dies, their interest passes automatically to the others and doesn't go through their will. (Yes, it's also the plot of the Simpsons' Flying Hellfish episode.)

Draft clauses

  1. Ownership. The vessel, trailer and gear are owned by the members as joint tenants. Interests: Merv 25%, Ace 25%, Bouncing 25%, Merv's son 12.5%, Northy 12.5% (Northy's interest takes effect when he signs — until then the others carry it).
  2. Survivorship. When a member dies, their interest passes to the surviving members in proportion to their shares. The last surviving member becomes sole owner.
  3. No exit. No member may sell, mortgage or give away their interest. Membership ends only by death (or by unanimous vote of the others).
  4. Pay your way. Costs are split by share through the CoPCO kitty. A member who's more than 6 months behind loses their vote on spending until they catch up. (Optional harder version: unpaid for 12 months = interest forfeits to the others.)
  5. Wills. Each member tells their family that the boat is not in their estate — it goes to the survivors.
  6. Rego holder. The boat is registered in one member's name as trustee for all members. If the rego holder dies, it's transferred to a surviving member.
  7. The ding rule. If someone damages the boat, they pay the insurance excess.
  8. Skippers. Members need a marine licence plus two supervised trips before taking her out without another member aboard.
  9. Disputes. Settled by a majority vote of the shares. A tie is settled over a beer at the Patterson River ramp.

Not legal advice. Joint tenancy can be "severed" (for example by a written notice from one owner, or by a bankruptcy), which would turn that share back into an ordinary saleable share. So have a solicitor turn this into a one-page signed agreement, and get every member to note it in their will. Cheap, worth it, and great for the merch.

Towing, ramps & the maiden voyage

🚙 Towing — what do we need?

A 5–7m trailer cabin cruiser plus motor, trailer, fuel and gear typically weighs 1.3–3.0 tonnes est.. The real number is on the trailer's compliance plate (ATM) — or take it over a weighbridge.

  • ~5–5.5m: roughly 1.3–1.8t · ~6–6.5m: 1.8–2.5t · 7m sterndrive: up to 3t.
  • Brakes: over 750kg needs brakes (override/hydraulic OK to 2t); over 2t needs a breakaway system.
  • Ball download: aim for ~10% of ATM. Too little = sway.
  • Licence: a normal car licence is fine within the vehicle's rated capacity.
  • Vehicles that'll do it comfortably: Ranger, D-Max, BT-50, LandCruiser (3,500kg); Prado/Hilux (~3,000kg). Mid-size SUVs (~1,600–2,000kg) are marginal.
Our rig: Caribbean Commodore + Yamaha V6 200 + Dunbier tandem ≈ 1,700–2,100 kg all up est.
2022 Kia Carnival diesel: 2,000 kg braked, 200 kg ball, front-wheel drive → borderline at best. Over the limit with fuel and gear, and FWD loses grip on a wet ramp. Weigh it at a weighbridge, and line up a Ranger/Prado/LandCruiser-class car for ramp days.

🧠 Hints & tricks

Before you tow
  • Lights + indicators work · safety chains crossed and shackled · coupling latched and pinned · jockey wheel up.
  • Wheel bearings — the #1 killer of old trailers. Check for play; repack yearly. Old tyres perish at 6–8 years regardless of tread (check the date code).
  • Tie-downs and transom straps on; winch strap not frayed; motor supported.
Reversing
  • Hand on the bottom of the wheel — move it the way you want the trailer to go. Small inputs, go slow, use a spotter.
  • Practise in an empty car park before ramp day. If it jack-knifes, pull forward and start again — nobody's judging (they are).
Ramp etiquette & launching
  1. Rig in the prep area, not on the ramp: straps off, gear in, lines on, BUNG IN.
  2. Let the hubs cool 10 minutes — hot bearings into cold water suck in water.
  3. Reverse down, someone holds the bow line, unclip the winch last.
  4. Float it off, walk it to the pontoon, clear the ramp, then park.
Retrieving & after
  • Drive/winch on to the stop, safety chain on, pull out and tidy up in the car park.
  • Pull the bung to drain. Flush the motor with fresh water (muffs on the hose) for 5–10 minutes after every salt trip, and hose the trailer.

🔧 What the marine mechanic should do

A boat that's been sitting for years needs a full recommission, not just a service. Hand this list over.

  • Fuel: drain the stale fuel, inspect the tank for water/sludge, replace fuel lines, primer bulb, filters and water separator. Clean or rebuild the carbs/injectors (varnish from old fuel is the usual culprit).
  • Cooling: new water-pump impeller, check thermostat, flush passages.
  • Gearbox/leg: change lower-unit oil — milky oil means water's getting in (seals).
  • Ignition: new plugs, check leads, compression test on each cylinder.
  • Electrics: battery load test, cables, terminals, fuses, kill switch, starter and solenoid.
  • Four-stroke: oil and filter. Sterndrive: bellows, gimbal bearing, outdrive oil.
  • Anodes, prop, steering, throttle and shift cables.
  • Trailer: bearings, tyres, brakes, lights.

⚡ No spark — next steps for the sparkie

  1. Battery stays above ~12V while cranking; lanyard clipped; key on; lever in neutral.
  2. Isolate the 703 control box: unplug the stop/key leads at the harness (check the wiring diagram first) and re-test for spark.
  3. Ohm-test the key switch and lanyard switch.
  4. Clean and reseat the main harness plug (corroded pins are common).
  5. Peak-voltage test the stator charge coil while cranking (needs a peak-voltage adapter; ~150V+).
  6. Test the pulser coils.
  7. V6 has two CDI packs (one per bank) — if one bank sparks, suspect the other pack.
  8. Still nothing? It's a mechanic job. Budget a full recommission at roughly $1,500–3,500; ignition parts or gearbox seals can push it past $4k.

Port Phillip Bay

Season guide

What to chase, when, and where — eastern and southern bay.

Where

  • Carrum / Patterson River: sand and weed in 4–10m — whiting, flathead, calamari. River mouth for salmon and gummies.
  • Frankston to Black Rock: eastern-shore snapper country.
  • Mornington & Mount Martha: snapper in 16–24m (14–16m at dawn); gummies near the shipping channel edge.
  • Safety Beach / Dromana: sand and weed patches for whiting and calamari.

Rigs that work

  • Snapper: running sinker to a swivel, 60–80cm trace, 5/0–7/0 circle hooks. Pilchard, squid, whiting heads. Berley. Soft plastics (4" pinkies, 5–7" big reds).
  • KG whiting: paternoster, pipi/mussel/squid strip, on a strong tide run over sand-and-weed edges.
  • Calamari: 2.5–3.5 egi jigs over weed beds, early and late, clean water.
  • Flathead: drift sand flats in 3–8m with plastics or bait.
  • Gummy shark: bottom rig, squid/pilchard/salmon fillet, dusk and night in 10–25m.

  • Stowage: under-bunk lockers, hull-side nets, a rod rack on the cabin roof lining.
  • 12V panel with USB sockets ($20–50) — phones, fish finder, speaker.
  • Look: white/grey lining, navy cushions with coral piping (CoPCO colours), warm-white LEDs, teak-look cup holders.

People have done this to boats just like ours. What it really costs to go all the way:

  • The Captain — "Old school Caribbean rebuild": two brothers in WA, a Caribbean Invader 520, 3 years. The stringers turned out to be completely rotten. ~$35k all-in with a Suzuki DF140, 38 knots.
  • Trade-a-Boat project boat — 1976 Caribbean Invader: transom repair + four-stroke repower, $32,450 with a full cost breakdown. Required reading before we start.
  • Ausfish — restored cruisers thread: before-and-after photos of Aussie half-cabins.
  • Bertram 20 rebuild: same builder, same construction — good on transoms and stringers.

The lesson: those big numbers are structural repairs plus a new motor. If our transom is sound and the V6 runs, the cabin refresh below costs a few hundred to a couple of thousand dollars.

📐 A cabin plan for our Commodore

Lock-up cabin, V-berth for two, toilet, little stove, hardtop with sliding windows. Do it in phases, out of the kitty:

  1. Strip & assess (a Saturday, ~$50): everything out — cushions, carpet, junk. Hose-test the hardtop and windows for leaks. Check the bunk bases and the floor for soft spots. Photograph everything.
  2. Clean & seal (~$150–300): mould wash, new window and hardtop seals, a cut-and-polish of the gelcoat.
  3. Lining (~$100–300 DIY): paint the fibreglass cabin sides white or grey, or glue thin ribbed marine carpet over ply panels.
  4. Cushions (~$250–650 DIY / $500–1,500 trimmer): new high-density foam with a waterproof liner, covered in navy marine vinyl with coral piping (the CoPCO colours). Make a filler cushion so the V-berth becomes a full bed.
  5. Toilet: keep it if it's a Porta Potti (empty it at home), or replace it with a Thetford 335 (~$200). No overboard discharge anywhere in Victoria.
  6. Galley box (~$150): a removable ply box for the stove, kettle and a small basin. Lift it out on fishing days.
  7. Lights & power (~$100–200): warm-white LED strips, a 12V/USB panel, a cup holder or four.
  8. The fridge (~$700–1,500): a 40–50L compressor fridge under the passenger seat, plus a second battery with a DC-DC charger.
  9. Finishing touches: CoPCO burgee on the bow rail, the new name on the hull, a framed photo of Uncle Uncle in the cabin.
